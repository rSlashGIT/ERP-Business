02
The Problem
Supply chain inventory management
Every day, a retailer must decide — order too little, lose sales; order too much, waste capital.

Retail inventory management is a daily optimization problem with asymmetric costs. Holding unsold stock incurs $0.05 per unit per day — capital cost, storage, obsolescence. An unmet unit of demand costs $3.00 in lost margin and customer goodwill.

The Newsvendor critical ratio is 3.00 / (3.00 + 0.05) = 98.4% — the optimal service level implied by the cost structure. Achieving it while adapting to demand volatility, lead times, and promotions is the core challenge.

The classical solution: the (s,S) policy. Reorder when inventory falls below threshold s. Order up to level S. Simple. Interpretable. Deployable. Dominant for 70 years.

Its weakness: static parameters cannot adapt to demand regime shifts, seasonal spikes, or changing cost structures. Two fixed numbers govern every decision — regardless of demand volatility, lead time, or seasonality.

The Method
Parameterized (s,S) + CMA-ES

Instead of replacing the (s,S) rule, we parameterized it. Eight weights let the reorder point s and order-up-to level S adapt to demand context — forecasts, volatility, lead times — while preserving the policy's interpretability and deployability.

The parameter vector θ = [s_base, S_base, w₁…w₆] initializes to the static (s,S) rule (all weights zero) and is then optimized by CMA-ES — Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy — a population-based, derivative-free optimizer that maintains a full covariance matrix over the search space.

Each CMA-ES generation samples 16 candidate policies, evaluates each over 64 training episodes on the M5 Walmart dataset (80% split, 1,553 rows), ranks by mean profit, and updates the covariance matrix toward the high-performing region of parameter space.

Training ran for up to 100 generations across 5 independent seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 999). Initial step-size σ = 5.0. Early stopping after 35 generations without improvement. The best θ per seed is preserved.

The result is a policy that looks identical to a classical (s,S) rule at inference time, but whose thresholds shift continuously with demand context — adapting without sacrificing explainability.
